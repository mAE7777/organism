Constraints enable creativity more often than they hinder it. The sonnet's 14-line structure in iambic pentameter doesn't restrict Shakespeare — it channels invention into density. Twitter's original 140-character limit produced a new literary form. When Stravinsky said "the more constraints one imposes, the more one frees oneself of the chains that shackle the spirit," he was describing the paradox that unlimited possibility produces paralysis while boundaries produce focus.

In engineering, constraints define the design space. Bridge engineers don't imagine ideal bridges — they solve for specific spans, load requirements, soil conditions, and budgets. The constraints eliminate infinite possibilities and leave a tractable problem. Often the tightest constraint drives the most innovative solution: weight limits on aircraft engines drove turbofan design improvements that also improved fuel efficiency, solving a problem nobody had directly targeted.

Biological constraints shape evolutionary innovation. The vertebrate body plan — bilateral symmetry, internal skeleton, four limbs — was set 400 million years ago. Every subsequent adaptation works within that constraint. Bat wings are modified hands; whale flippers are modified arms. Evolution can't redesign from scratch, so it repurposes existing structures. This constraint produces solutions no engineer would design intentionally, but that work remarkably well given the materials available.
