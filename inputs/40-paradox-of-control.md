The tighter you grip, the less you control. Centralized command economies generated five-year plans with extraordinary detail but couldn't adapt to local conditions or unexpected changes. The Soviet Union could mobilize millions for steel production but couldn't reliably stock grocery stores with fresh vegetables. Decentralized market systems, with no central controller, solve the vegetable problem daily through price signals that aggregate distributed local knowledge no planner could collect.

In management, micromanagement degrades the performance it seeks to ensure. When managers dictate methods rather than outcomes, they remove the employee's autonomy to adapt to conditions the manager can't see. Hackman's research on team effectiveness shows that the most productive teams have clear goals and constraints but freedom in execution. The leader's job is to set the boundary conditions and then resist the urge to intervene.

Ecological management reveals the same paradox. Fire suppression attempts to control wildfire and produces worse wildfires. Straightening rivers for flood control accelerates water flow, increasing downstream flooding. Pesticide application eliminates pest predators, requiring more pesticide. In each case, the control intervention disrupts the feedback loops that maintained the original equilibrium. The system resists control by reorganizing around the intervention, often in ways that amplify the original problem.
