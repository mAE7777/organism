Rebalancing forces the counterintuitive discipline of selling winners and buying losers. When equities surge, they become overweighted in a portfolio — rebalancing trims them back to target allocation, effectively capturing gains. When bonds crash, rebalancing buys more at depressed prices. The mechanism works because asset classes mean-revert over long horizons, though the timing is unpredictable. You don't need to predict; you need a systematic rule that exploits reversion automatically.

Calendar rebalancing (quarterly, annually) is simple but suboptimal. Threshold rebalancing — triggering only when allocations drift beyond a band (say 5%) — reduces unnecessary trading costs while still capturing large dislocations. Vanguard's research shows threshold-based approaches produce similar returns with roughly 40% fewer trades. The savings compound over decades, especially in taxable accounts where each rebalancing event can trigger capital gains.

Tax-loss harvesting interacts with rebalancing in subtle ways. Selling a depreciated asset to realize a loss, then rebalancing into a correlated but not identical replacement, captures tax benefits without abandoning the allocation strategy. The wash-sale rule demands 30 days of separation between selling and repurchasing substantially identical securities — a constraint that requires careful choreography across accounts.
