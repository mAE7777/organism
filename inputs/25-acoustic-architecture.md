Concert hall acoustics are measured by reverberation time (RT60) — the seconds needed for sound to decay by 60 decibels. Vienna's Musikverein, widely regarded as the world's finest hall, has an RT60 of about 2.0 seconds. Boston Symphony Hall, designed by Wallace Sabine using his pioneering acoustic equations, targets similar values. Too short feels dead; too long turns music into mush. The ideal depends on repertoire — Baroque demands clarity (shorter RT60), Romantic demands warmth (longer).

Diffusion matters as much as absorption. Flat parallel walls create flutter echoes — rapid repetitions that degrade clarity. The Elbphilharmonie in Hamburg uses algorithmically designed wall panels with thousands of unique surface angles that scatter sound in all directions. Each seat receives sound from multiple reflected paths, arriving within milliseconds, creating a sense of immersion that no amplification system can replicate.

The inverse square law means that direct sound drops 6dB every time distance doubles. In large halls, reflected sound quickly dominates over direct sound beyond the first few rows. Acoustic designers use this to their advantage: early reflections (arriving within 50ms of direct sound) are perceived as part of the original source, adding loudness and spatial richness without perceived echo.
