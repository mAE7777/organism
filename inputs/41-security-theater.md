Security theater — visible measures that provide psychological reassurance without meaningfully reducing risk — is widely mocked by security professionals. But deterrence operates on perception, not reality. A visible security camera that records nothing still changes behavior because potential offenders can't distinguish it from a functional one. The TSA's liquid ban may not stop a sophisticated attacker, but it raises the perceived cost of attempting an attack, redirecting effort toward softer targets. Deterrence doesn't require effectiveness; it requires credibility.

The mere presence of security personnel reduces crime through the observer effect, regardless of their actual capabilities. Studies of uniformed guards in retail show 25-40% reductions in shoplifting, even when the guards have no authority to detain. The mechanism is social, not tactical: visible security signals that someone is watching, activating the same norm-compliance behavior that empty police cars parked by highways trigger in speeding drivers.

Security theater also serves a legitimate organizational function: it communicates that an institution takes safety seriously, maintaining public trust that enables normal operations. An airport without visible screening would hemorrhage passengers regardless of its actual safety record. Institutions need functional security and performed security — the performance isn't dishonesty; it's the visible layer of a social contract that keeps systems operating under conditions of uncertainty.
