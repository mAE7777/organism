Heidegger's concept of Gestell (enframing) argues that modern technology doesn't just use nature — it reveals nature as standing reserve, raw material awaiting extraction. A river isn't water flowing; it's a hydroelectric resource. This reframing isn't evil, but it's totalizing: once you see the world as resource, the old way of seeing becomes inaccessible. The danger isn't any particular technology but the mode of thinking that technology installs.

Illich's concept of conviviality distinguishes tools that extend human autonomy from those that replace it. A bicycle amplifies human locomotion; a freeway system restructures cities around cars, making non-car life progressively impossible. The test isn't whether a tool is useful but whether it remains optional — whether people who don't adopt it are merely less efficient or effectively excluded from participation.

Winner's concept of technological politics shows that artifacts embed values. Robert Moses's low bridge overpasses on Long Island parkways prevented buses from reaching Jones Beach — a physical architecture that encoded racial and class exclusion. Technologies aren't neutral instruments wielded by biased humans; the bias can be baked into the infrastructure itself, persisting long after the designer's intent is forgotten.
