Monocultures are ecologically fragile, but they're economically and logistically formidable. A thousand-hectare wheat field can be planted, fertilized, sprayed, and harvested with identical machinery on identical schedules. Polyculture requires crop-specific equipment, staggered timing, and agronomic knowledge that scales poorly. The Green Revolution fed billions precisely because monoculture enabled mechanization, which enabled the caloric output that prevented the famines Ehrlich predicted in the 1960s. Diversity is a luxury that sufficient calories make possible.

Standardization — monoculture's abstract equivalent — enables interoperability that diversity cannot. USB-C replaced a chaos of proprietary connectors. Container shipping works because every container is the same dimensions. TCP/IP succeeded because everyone agreed on one protocol stack. In each case, the monoculture solution eliminated the friction of translation between diverse standards, producing system-level efficiency gains that no individual diverse component could match.

Biological monocultures can be strategically optimal in controlled environments. Greenhouse tomato production uses single cultivars selected for yield, disease resistance, and uniformity because the controlled environment eliminates most stresses that diversity hedges against. When you can control temperature, humidity, nutrients, and pathogens, the insurance value of genetic diversity drops toward zero and the efficiency gains of uniformity dominate. The problem with monoculture isn't the monoculture — it's the mismatch between uniformity and uncontrolled environments.
