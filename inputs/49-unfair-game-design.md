Perfect balance in competitive games produces stagnation. When every option is equally viable, no strategic exploration is needed — any choice performs identically. Intentional imbalance creates a metagame: players discover dominant strategies, opponents develop counters, the counter gets countered, and the cycle drives continuous innovation. Street Fighter's tier lists aren't a design failure; they're the engine of competitive evolution. A perfectly balanced game would have no tiers, no adaptation pressure, and no reason to experiment.

Asymmetric starting conditions produce richer strategic spaces than symmetric ones. StarCraft's three races — Terran, Zerg, Protoss — are deliberately different in economy, unit design, and playstyle. This asymmetry makes the game harder to balance but creates matchups where each combination demands distinct strategies. Chess gives White a measurable first-move advantage (White wins ~55% of decisive games), and this asymmetry creates the dynamic tension between White's initiative and Black's counterplay that makes the game compelling.

Randomness — the ultimate "unfairness" — serves essential design functions. Poker would be trivially solvable without hidden cards. Backgammon's dice prevent the better player from winning every game, which keeps weaker players engaged and learning. Even in competitive esports, random elements (critical hits, spawn points) prevent memorization from fully replacing adaptation. Complete fairness produces deterministic outcomes that destroy the uncertainty necessary for engagement. Some unfairness is what makes a game worth playing.
