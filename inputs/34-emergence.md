Emergence describes properties that exist at the system level but not at the component level. A single neuron doesn't think; a hundred billion neurons connected by trillions of synapses produce consciousness (or something functionally equivalent). The interesting question isn't whether emergence is "real" — it's why certain arrangements of simple components reliably produce complex behavior. Conway's Game of Life generates gliders, oscillators, and Turing-complete computation from four rules on a grid.

Weak emergence is compressible — you can derive the macro behavior from micro rules, even if the derivation is computationally expensive. Temperature emerges from molecular kinetic energy; given enough computing power, you could simulate every molecule. Strong emergence, if it exists, would mean macro properties that are in principle irreducible. Whether consciousness qualifies remains philosophy's hardest question.

Phase transitions are emergence's most dramatic form. Water molecules don't individually "decide" to become ice at 0C. The transition is a collective phenomenon — a sudden reorganization of the entire system at a critical threshold. Markets crash the same way: individual sell decisions aggregate into a cascade that no single participant intended or could have prevented. The transition point is a property of the network, not any node.
