Food security has four dimensions: availability, access, utilization, and stability. A country can produce enough calories (availability) while millions go hungry because they can't afford to buy food (access). India simultaneously has grain surpluses rotting in government warehouses and the world's largest undernourished population. The problem isn't production — it's distribution infrastructure and purchasing power.

The Green Revolution traded resilience for yield. High-yielding wheat and rice varieties developed by Borlaug and IRRI fed billions but required irrigation, fertilizer, and pesticides that smallholder farmers often couldn't afford. Genetic uniformity also created vulnerability: when a new disease appears, it can sweep through genetically identical crops across entire regions. The Irish Potato Famine resulted from exactly this kind of monoculture dependency.

Seed sovereignty movements argue that corporate consolidation of seed supply — four companies control over 60% of global commercial seed sales — creates systemic risk. When farmers depend on annual seed purchases rather than saving and replanting, the food system acquires a single point of failure. Traditional landraces maintained by subsistence farmers represent a distributed genetic library that no seed vault can fully replicate.
