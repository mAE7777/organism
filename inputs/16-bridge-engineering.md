The Tacoma Narrows collapse in 1940 taught engineers that static load calculations are insufficient. The bridge could handle its weight — it couldn't handle wind-induced aeroelastic flutter. The failure mode wasn't overload but resonance: periodic forces coupling with the structure's natural frequency until oscillations became self-reinforcing. Modern bridge design now incorporates wind tunnel testing and tuned mass dampers to break these feedback loops.

Redundancy in bridge design follows a principle called damage tolerance. Cable-stayed bridges like the Millau Viaduct are designed so that any single cable can fail without triggering progressive collapse. The remaining cables absorb redistributed loads while repairs proceed. This isn't over-engineering — it's engineering for the inevitable degradation of real materials under real conditions.

Suspension bridges exploit a counterintuitive property of catenary curves: the cable's shape naturally distributes load evenly across the span. The main cables don't fight gravity — they redirect it. Vertical suspender cables transfer deck weight upward to the main cable, which transfers it laterally to the anchorages. The entire structure is in tension, not compression.
