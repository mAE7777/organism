Catalysis is chemistry's version of leverage. A platinum surface doesn't get consumed in catalytic conversion — it provides a lower-energy pathway for reactions that would otherwise require extreme heat or pressure. Enzymes do the same biologically: lysozyme breaks bacterial cell walls by stabilizing the transition state, reducing the activation energy by a factor of ten million. The catalyst doesn't change what's thermodynamically possible; it changes what's kinetically accessible.

Le Chatelier's principle governs equilibrium: stress a system and it shifts to counteract the change. Add more reactant, and the system produces more product. Raise temperature on an exothermic reaction, and it shifts backward. Industrial ammonia synthesis (Haber process) exploits this — high pressure and moderate temperature push nitrogen and hydrogen toward ammonia, while an iron catalyst makes it happen at practical rates.

Self-assembly in chemistry shows how complexity arises without blueprints. Lipid molecules in water spontaneously form bilayer membranes because their hydrophobic tails minimize contact with water. No instruction set, no external organizer — just local thermodynamic preferences producing globally ordered structures that happen to be the foundation of all cellular life.
