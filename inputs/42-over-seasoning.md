The culinary orthodoxy that seasoning should "enhance, not overpower" the main ingredient assumes that the main ingredient is always worth enhancing. Fermentation-heavy cuisines like Korean and West African cooking deliberately obliterate the base flavor of bland staples — rice, cassava, millet — under layers of gochujang, dawadawa, and fermented shrimp paste. The seasoning isn't supporting the grain; the grain is a delivery vehicle for the seasoning. This is a valid culinary philosophy, not a failure of restraint.

Capsaicin tolerance shifts the hedonic response to spicy food from pain to pleasure, opening flavor dimensions inaccessible to the uninitiated. Sichuan cuisine's mala (numbing-hot) combination creates a tactile experience that interacts with taste in ways that mild food simply cannot produce. The argument that heavy seasoning "masks" flavor assumes a single correct level of flavor intensity. In practice, intensity is a dimension, not a flaw — orchestral music isn't "masking" the oboe.

Salt at concentrations that would seem aggressive to modern Western palates was standard in Roman and medieval cooking for a reason: it triggers umami synergy, enhances sweetness, suppresses bitterness, and extends preservation. The 20th-century reduction in salt was driven by public health concerns about hypertension, not by culinary logic. From a pure flavor perspective, most home cooks under-season — professional chefs consistently use 2-3x more salt than home recipes suggest.
