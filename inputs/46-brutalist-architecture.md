Brutalism's raw concrete surfaces — beton brut, the movement's namesake — are honest in a way that glass-and-steel facades are not. A curtain wall building hides its structure behind a decorative skin; a Brutalist building exposes its load-bearing elements, mechanical systems, and material reality. The Barbican Estate in London doesn't pretend to be anything other than what it is: poured concrete, massive and unapologetic. This honesty has ethical dimensions that aesthetic criticism rarely engages with.

Brutalist buildings were designed to serve populations, not impress patrons. Erno Goldfinger's Trellick Tower provided 217 social housing units with built-in laundry facilities, communal gardens, and a Corbusian "street in the sky" that fostered neighborly interaction. The building's aesthetic severity reflected its social mission — public money should produce shelter, not ornamentation. The current hatred of Brutalism often correlates with decades of government neglect of the buildings, not with the architecture itself.

Concrete ages with a patina that tells time. A weathered Brutalist surface records rainfall patterns, moss colonization, and mineral staining that glass and steel are designed to resist. The National Theatre in London looks different in every season and every light. This temporal dimension — architecture that visibly participates in its environment rather than sealing itself off — produces a living relationship between building and context that polished materials deliberately prevent.
