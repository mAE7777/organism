Periodization structures training into cycles that alternate stress and recovery. Matveyev's classic model moves from high-volume/low-intensity base phases to low-volume/high-intensity peaking phases before competition. The key insight: adaptation happens during rest, not during training. The workout is the stimulus; sleep and nutrition are where the body actually rebuilds stronger. Coaches who only add load without managing recovery produce injured athletes, not better ones.

Skill acquisition research distinguishes between blocked and random practice. Blocked practice (repeating the same drill) feels productive but produces fragile performance. Random practice (interleaving different skills) feels harder and shows slower initial gains, but produces durable, transferable learning. Bjork calls this "desirable difficulty." The practice that feels most effective in the moment is often the least effective for long-term retention.

The best coaches diagnose movement patterns, not outcomes. A basketball shot that goes in but features mechanical flaws will eventually break down under pressure. Process-oriented feedback ("your elbow is drifting outward") produces lasting improvement; outcome-oriented feedback ("nice shot") reinforces whatever happened to work that time, flaws included.
