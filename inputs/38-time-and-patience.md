Compound interest is unintuitive because human cognition underestimates exponential growth. Warren Buffett accumulated 99% of his wealth after age 50 — not because his returns improved, but because compounding needs time to dominate. A 10% annual return doubles capital every 7.2 years. The first doubling feels negligible; the tenth is transformative. Most investors abandon strategies that are working slowly in favor of strategies that promise to work quickly, sacrificing the very mechanism that generates long-term wealth.

In ecology, old-growth forests take 200-500 years to develop their full complexity — cathedral canopies, nurse logs, multi-layered understories, and the deep soil mycorrhizal networks that support everything. You can plant a forest in a day. You cannot accelerate the processes that convert a plantation into an ecosystem. Some complexity requires duration, not intensity. Time itself is an irreplaceable input.

Mastery follows a similar curve. Ericsson's deliberate practice research suggests expert-level performance requires roughly 10,000 hours — not because the number is magic, but because the neural remodeling that underlies deep skill takes years of consistent stimulus. The frustrating implication: there are no shortcuts. Techniques that accelerate early learning (spaced repetition, interleaving) improve efficiency but don't eliminate the fundamental requirement of sustained engagement over years.
