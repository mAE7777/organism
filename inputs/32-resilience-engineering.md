Resilience engineering, as defined by Hollnagel and Woods, studies how systems succeed under varying conditions rather than just why they fail. Traditional safety analysis is retrospective — investigating accidents after they happen. Resilience engineering asks: what allows the system to function normally despite constant perturbation? The answer is usually human adaptability — operators compensating for design gaps, improvising workarounds, and absorbing variation that the formal system never anticipated.

The concept of Safety-II reframes human variability from error source to resilience source. In the Air France 447 crash, investigators blamed pilot error. A resilience perspective asks why thousands of similar encounters with unreliable airspeed data didn't crash — because pilots adapted successfully. The failures are visible; the daily successes that share the same underlying conditions are invisible. Studying success reveals the adaptive capacity that studying failure alone cannot.

Graceful degradation distinguishes resilient systems from brittle ones. A brittle system works perfectly until it fails catastrophically — a cliff edge. A resilient system degrades gradually, maintaining core functions while shedding less critical ones. Hospital emergency departments during surge events triage patients, delay elective procedures, and convert hallways to treatment areas. The system gets worse, but it doesn't break.
