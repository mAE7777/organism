Positive feedback amplifies change; negative feedback dampens it. Both are essential. A microphone near a speaker creates positive feedback — the howling screech of runaway amplification. But positive feedback also drives useful exponential processes: compound interest, viral adoption, and the ice-albedo effect where melting ice exposes dark ocean that absorbs more heat, accelerating further melting. The loop itself is neither good nor bad; it's the context that determines whether amplification is constructive or destructive.

Negative feedback loops are the foundation of homeostasis. A thermostat measures temperature, compares it to a setpoint, and activates heating or cooling to close the gap. Blood sugar regulation, cruise control, and market price discovery all follow the same pattern: sense, compare, adjust. The delay between sensing and adjusting determines stability — too much delay produces oscillation, as the system overshoots and overcorrects.

Coupled feedback loops create complexity that single loops cannot. Predator-prey dynamics involve two interlocked negative feedback loops with delays — more rabbits enable more foxes, which reduce rabbits, which reduce foxes. The result is oscillation, not equilibrium. Lotka-Volterra equations model this, but real ecosystems add hundreds of such coupled loops, producing dynamics that resist simple prediction and demand simulation or observation rather than analytical solutions.
