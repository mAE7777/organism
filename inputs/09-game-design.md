The best game mechanics create emergent complexity from simple rules. Chess has six piece types and one objective, yet produces more possible games than atoms in the universe. Depth comes from interaction between rules, not from the rules themselves.

Flow state in games requires balancing challenge against skill. Too easy produces boredom; too hard produces anxiety. The sweet spot is a narrow channel that shifts as the player improves. Great games dynamically adjust difficulty — not by changing rules, but by introducing new contexts that demand existing skills in novel combinations.

Loss aversion shapes game design more than reward. Players feel losses roughly twice as intensely as equivalent gains. This asymmetry means that taking something away (even temporarily) is a more powerful design tool than giving something new. The threat of loss creates engagement that pure reward cannot match.
