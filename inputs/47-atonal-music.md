Atonality didn't destroy musical meaning — it expanded the vocabulary of expression beyond what tonal harmony could articulate. Schoenberg's "Pierrot Lunaire" uses Sprechstimme (speech-singing) and free atonality to express states of psychological disintegration that a major-minor framework literally cannot represent. Tonality implies resolution; atonality suspends it indefinitely. For a generation that lived through the trenches of World War I, music that promised resolution would have been a lie.

The twelve-tone method provides structure more rigorous than tonal harmony, not less. A tone row is a specific permutation of all twelve chromatic pitches, and Schoenberg's rules for its manipulation (retrograde, inversion, transposition) create a compositional logic as strict as counterpoint. Berg's Violin Concerto embeds tonal triads within a twelve-tone row, producing music that is simultaneously serial and deeply expressive — proof that the system doesn't preclude beauty, only conventional beauty.

Audiences who reject atonal music often reject unfamiliarity, not quality. Stravinsky's "Rite of Spring" provoked riots at its 1913 premiere; it's now standard orchestral repertoire performed for audiences who find it thrilling rather than threatening. Exposure effect and cultural habituation determine the boundary of acceptable dissonance. Medieval listeners found thirds dissonant; Renaissance listeners found them consonant. The boundary moves. What sounds like noise today may sound like expression tomorrow — and has, repeatedly, throughout music history.
