Diagnostic AI systems like Google's retinal screening model can detect diabetic retinopathy with sensitivity exceeding most ophthalmologists. But deployment in Thai clinics revealed a gap: the model was trained on high-quality fundus images, while real-world photos from overworked nurses in dim rooms produced artifacts the model couldn't handle. It rejected 21% of images as ungradeable. Technical accuracy means nothing if the system can't tolerate the noise of actual clinical environments.

Explainability in medical AI isn't a nice-to-have — it's a regulatory and clinical necessity. A black-box model that correctly flags a tumor but can't indicate which features triggered the alert forces clinicians to choose between trusting the machine blindly and ignoring it entirely. Grad-CAM and SHAP values provide heatmaps of model attention, but whether these explanations map to genuine causal reasoning remains an open question.

Dataset bias propagates into clinical harm. Dermatology AI trained predominantly on light-skinned patients underperforms on darker skin tones — precisely the populations already underserved by the healthcare system. The training data encodes historical access patterns, and the model faithfully reproduces those inequities at scale, faster and more consistently than any individual physician's bias could.
