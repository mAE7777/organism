Post-fire recovery follows a predictable succession, but the speed depends on fire intensity and soil damage. Low-severity fires that burn understory but leave the canopy intact may recover within a decade. Stand-replacing fires that kill mature trees reset the clock to pioneer species — fireweed, lodgepole pine, aspen — which colonize through wind-dispersed seeds or fire-activated serotinous cones that only open under extreme heat. The catastrophe is the seed's trigger.

Soil mycorrhizal networks suffer massively in high-severity fires. The top centimeters of soil, where fungal hyphae concentrate, can reach 500C. Recovery of these underground networks takes 10-20 years and limits how quickly trees can reestablish. Transplanting nursery seedlings with intact root fungi accelerates recovery dramatically — the bottleneck isn't the tree, it's the symbiotic partner the tree needs to access soil nutrients.

Fire suppression creates a paradox. A century of preventing small fires in western US forests allowed fuel loads to accumulate — ladder fuels connecting ground to canopy. When fire inevitably arrives, it burns hotter and wider than any historical precedent. Prescribed burns remove fuel incrementally, accepting small controlled damage to prevent catastrophic uncontrolled damage. The system needs regular small disturbances to avoid rare large ones.
