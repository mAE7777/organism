Index funds are celebrated as the triumph of humility over hubris — accept market returns instead of trying to beat them. But universal indexing creates a paradox: index funds are free riders on the price discovery work of active managers. If everyone indexes, nobody researches companies, prices stop reflecting fundamentals, and the market becomes a random walk disconnected from economic reality. The efficiency that indexing exploits depends on active managers that indexing is slowly eliminating.

Market-cap weighting, the default index construction method, systematically overweights overvalued stocks and underweights undervalued ones. When a stock's price rises above its fundamental value, its index weight increases, forcing index funds to buy more of it — a positive feedback loop that amplifies mispricing. The 2000 dot-com bubble saw index funds pouring money into Cisco at 150x earnings because its market cap demanded it. Indexing doesn't avoid bubbles; it mechanically participates in them.

Concentration risk in cap-weighted indices has intensified dramatically. The top 10 stocks in the S&P 500 now represent over 35% of the index — levels not seen since the early 1970s. An investor who buys "the whole market" through an S&P 500 index fund is making a concentrated bet on a handful of mega-cap tech companies. The diversification that indexing promises is increasingly nominal. The market portfolio is narrower than it appears.
