Shannon's fundamental theorem established that every communication channel has a maximum reliable data rate (capacity), and you can get arbitrarily close to it with sufficiently clever encoding. The key insight isn't about speed — it's that noise doesn't destroy the possibility of communication; it just sets a ceiling. Error-correcting codes like Reed-Solomon don't eliminate errors; they add structured redundancy that lets the receiver reconstruct the original message despite corruption. Reliability emerges from intelligent redundancy, not from eliminating noise.

Entropy in information theory measures surprise, not disorder. A message from an alphabet where all symbols are equiprobable has maximum entropy — each symbol carries maximum information because it's maximally unpredictable. English text has low entropy because letter frequencies are skewed (e appears 13% of the time, z appears 0.07%). Compression algorithms exploit this — they assign shorter codes to common patterns and longer codes to rare ones.

The rate-distortion theorem formalizes a universal tradeoff: lossy compression can achieve smaller file sizes than lossless, but only by accepting some distortion. JPEG discards high-frequency visual details that human eyes barely perceive. The theorem says you can't do better than a mathematically defined boundary — every compression scheme faces the same fundamental tradeoff between fidelity and compactness.
