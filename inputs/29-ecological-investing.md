ESG scoring attempts to quantify environmental, social, and governance risk, but correlation between rating agencies is only about 0.6 — compared to 0.99 for credit ratings. MSCI and Sustainalytics can give the same company radically different ESG scores because they weight different factors and use different measurement methodologies. This isn't a solvable data problem; it reflects genuine disagreement about what sustainability means and how to measure externalities.

Carbon offset markets reveal the complexity of pricing ecological damage. A ton of CO2 removed via direct air capture costs $600+; a ton "avoided" through rainforest preservation might cost $10. Are these equivalent? The permanence, additionality, and verification challenges differ enormously. Cheap offsets may represent real carbon benefits or elaborate accounting fictions — distinguishing them requires ecological expertise that financial analysts typically lack.

Impact investing's theory of change assumes that capital allocation can drive environmental outcomes. But the mechanism is indirect: buying shares of a green company on the secondary market doesn't give that company money; it raises their stock price, which may lower their cost of future capital. The actual impact chain has more links and more leakage than most impact reports acknowledge.
