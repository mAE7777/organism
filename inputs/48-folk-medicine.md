Aspirin derives from willow bark, metformin from French lilac, artemisinin from sweet wormwood. These aren't coincidences — they're the products of centuries of empirical observation by practitioners who lacked germ theory but not pattern recognition. Folk medicine traditions represent distributed clinical trials conducted over generations, with remedies that survived because they produced observable effects. The selection mechanism wasn't peer review; it was mortality. Treatments that didn't work were abandoned by communities that couldn't afford to maintain useless practices.

Traditional Chinese Medicine's classification of herbs by thermal properties (hot, cold, warm, cool) maps imperfectly but nontrivially onto modern pharmacological categories. "Cooling" herbs frequently contain anti-inflammatory compounds; "warming" herbs often contain vasodilators or metabolic stimulants. The folk taxonomy captured real physiological effects using a conceptual framework available at the time. Dismissing the framework doesn't invalidate the observations embedded within it.

Ethnobotanical knowledge is disappearing faster than the species it describes. When an Amazonian shaman dies without apprentices, the community loses not just cultural heritage but a curated pharmacopoeia refined over millennia. Pharmaceutical companies screen thousands of compounds for one viable drug; indigenous practitioners have already performed that screening through generations of use. The remaining uncontacted peoples may harbor botanical knowledge with direct pharmaceutical value that no laboratory screening program could replicate.
