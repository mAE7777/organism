Byzantine fault tolerance addresses the hardest trust problem: how do nodes reach consensus when some participants may be actively malicious? The Byzantine Generals Problem, formulated by Lamport in 1982, proved that reliable consensus requires at least 3f+1 nodes to tolerate f Byzantine faults. Bitcoin's proof-of-work sidesteps this by making deception computationally expensive rather than impossible — you don't need to trust any individual miner, only that the majority of computing power is honest.

Trust in human systems works differently but shares structural parallels. Reputation systems on eBay and Airbnb convert repeated interactions into trust scores that enable transactions between strangers. The mechanism is historical: past behavior predicts future behavior. But cold-start problems persist — new participants have no history, creating a bootstrapping dilemma that platforms solve with verification, deposits, or guarantees that substitute institutional trust for personal trust.

Certificate authorities in TLS represent hierarchical trust — you trust a website because you trust the CA that vouched for it, because your browser vendor trusted that CA. This chain concentrates risk: a compromised CA (like DigiNotar in 2011) breaks trust for every certificate it ever issued. The alternative, web-of-trust models like PGP, distribute trust but require active participation that most users won't sustain. Every trust architecture trades one vulnerability for another.
