Ecosystems don't optimize — they adapt. There's no central controller deciding which species thrives. Instead, millions of local interactions create emergent stability. Remove a keystone species and the whole system reorganizes, sometimes catastrophically.

Mycorrhizal networks — fungal threads connecting tree roots — transfer nutrients from healthy trees to struggling ones. The forest isn't a collection of competing individuals; it's a distributed system where resources flow toward need. Old trees subsidize young ones through underground channels that took decades to establish.

Succession is ecology's version of creative destruction. After a fire clears a forest, pioneer species colonize the bare ground. They're fast-growing, sun-loving, and short-lived. They create shade, which enables the next wave of slower, shade-tolerant species. The pioneers engineer the conditions for their own replacement.
